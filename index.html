    EXPLORE AFTERLIGHT

    How to use the app

    Follow one country's lights and economy through twenty years.

    1. Choose a country from the menu or click it on the map. The map zooms to that country and its review starts at 2004.
    2. Press play to move through the years, or drag the year slider to a year you want to see. The country stays selected. Watch where its night lights gather, grow or fade.
    3. Read the left panel. It shows the change in total night lights since 2004, GDP for the selected year, and its change since 2004 when figures are available.
    4. Follow the two lines. Gold shows total night lights; violet shows GDP. Both start at 100 in their first shared year. A value of 200 means double the starting amount.

    Press pause to take a closer look. Moving the map also pauses playback. Choose another country to begin a new review at 2004.
    READING AFTERLIGHT

    What the lights can tell us.

    Explore how night lights and GDP changed across Africa from 2004 to 2024. Choose a country, then play or move through its years.

    Where the lights come from

    We use older and newer satellite records to show how night lights changed over time. Earlier years include estimates based on older records. Each map shows an average for the whole year, not a single night.

    How we measure the lights

    We group the satellite readings into cells about 7.4 km wide at the equator. This makes the map easier to explore, but it can miss small patches of light. Small islands and places near country borders are less precise.

    A cluster is a group of bright cells that touch, including at their corners. We use the same brightness cutoff every year. A cluster can cross a border; each country is measured using the part inside its boundary.

    • Brightness: the average strength of the light.
    • Lit area: the land covered by cells above the brightness cutoff.
    • Concentration: how much of a country's lit area belongs to its largest cluster.
    • Coverage: how much land has usable readings. Lit share tells us how much of that land is bright enough to count as lit.
    • Missing data: readings that are unavailable are left out, not treated as zero.

    Calculations account for the area each cell covers. The map uses the same colour scale every year, so a brighter colour always has the same meaning.

    Reading the GDP comparison

    GDP describes the value of goods and services produced in a country. The figures use 2015 prices to make years easier to compare without the effect of inflation.

    On the chart, both lines start at 100 in the first year with data for both. A value of 200 means twice the starting amount. The Africa-wide chart compares the same set of countries throughout.

    The detailed comparison checks whether yearly changes in lights and GDP tend to move together. It needs at least 10 matching year-to-year changes. Moving together does not prove that one caused the other. Lights can also come from industry and gas flaring, and lighting technology changes over time.

    Country boundaries

    We use Natural Earth boundaries consistently across all years. Western Sahara appears for context but is not included in country rankings. Somaliland is included with Somalia. These mapping choices do not express a position on disputed boundaries.

    Abbreviations

    GDP
    Gross Domestic Product: the value of goods and services produced in a country.
    VIIRS
    Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite: the satellite instrument used for newer night-light observations.
    DMSP
    Defense Meteorological Satellite Program: the older satellite programme used to reconstruct earlier years.
    WDI
    World Development Indicators: the World Bank's collection of development statistics.
    USD / US$
    United States dollars.
    nW/cm²/sr
    Nanowatts per square centimetre per steradian: the unit used to measure the strength of satellite-observed light.
    ρ (rho)
    The symbol for the correlation score. Positive values mean changes tend to move together; negative values mean they tend to move in opposite directions.
    PNG
    Portable Network Graphics: the image format used for the map's yearly light layers.
